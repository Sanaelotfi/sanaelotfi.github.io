Quantized Reasoning Models Think They Need to Think Longer, but They Do Not

A token-level look at why post-training quantization makes reasoning models overthink.

Sanae Lotfi1, Polina Kirichenko1, Steven Li2, Zechun Liu2
1FAIR at Meta   2Meta AI
Paper

TL;DR. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is widely used to deploy large reasoning models efficiently. We find that aggressive PTQ does not just lower accuracy. It causes overthinking: the quantized model reaches the correct answer in an intermediate reasoning step, then opens new branches, second-guesses itself, and never commits. In up to 52% of 3-bit AWQ failures on MATH-500, the answer was already there. Token-level KL divergence between the BF16 and quantized models concentrates on hesitation tokens (“Wait”, “But”, “Alternatively”), at exactly the decoding positions where the BF16 model was already uncertain.

Aggressive PTQ lowers accuracy and inflates chains of thought

We evaluate five reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen 1.5B / 7B / 14B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama 8B, and QwQ-32B), three PTQ methods (GPTQ, AWQ, FlatQuant), and five benchmarks (AIME-120, MATH-500, GSM8K, GPQA-Diamond, LiveCodeBench). Mild quantization is essentially free. Pushing to 3-bit weights or W4A4KV4 hurts in both directions at once: accuracy drops and chains of thought balloon.

DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B across three benchmarks. On MATH-500, 3-bit AWQ drops accuracy from 85.6% to 47.0% while inflating CoT from 5.2K to 23.4K tokens (4.5×).

The pattern is consistent across all 28 model and quantization pairs we run. The Spearman correlation between accuracy loss and CoT length increase is ρ = -0.73. The models that lose the most accuracy generate the longest chains, which suggests that the extra reasoning is not just a co-occurring symptom of failure. It looks like a cause of it.

The failure mode is overthinking, not lost capability

We define an overthinking error as a failure where the model reaches the correct answer at some point in its chain of thought, then opens new reasoning paths, questions its own assumptions, or reverses the correct conclusion before producing a final answer. We hand-annotated a subset of MATH-500 failures and used GPT-5 (calibrated to over 95% agreement with our annotation) to scale labels into four buckets: overthinking, logical error, arithmetic error, and formatting / hallucination / other.

If quantization were a uniform capability hit, all four error categories would grow in proportion. They do not. Overthinking errors inflate disproportionately while arithmetic and logical errors stay roughly flat. The quantized model is not losing its math. It is losing its ability to stop. Quantized reasoning models do not fail because they cannot think. They fail because they cannot stop thinking.

Where exactly do the two models diverge?

To localize the failure, we run BF16 and 3-bit AWQ on the same MATH-500 prompts under identical generation prefixes, then measure per-position KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_t \,\|\, q_t)$ between the BF16 and quantized next-token distributions. KL divergence is a standard measure of how much one distribution differs from another. Large KL at a position means the quantized and full-precision models disagree sharply about what to say next.

We then associate each KL value with the token the quantized model actually sampled at that position, and average across all positions where each unique token appears (with a minimum count of 50).

Three things fall out:

  • Highest-KL tokens are hesitation or branching tokens (“Wait”, “But”, “Alternatively”, “maybe”, “verify”, “think”). Many of them open new reasoning paths or express doubt.
  • Lowest-KL tokens are mathematical and formatting tokens. Quantization barely changes how the model samples digits, operators, or LaTeX markup.
  • Position-level KL tracks BF16 next-token entropy almost perfectly (ρ = 0.92). Quantization noise has the most leverage at positions where the BF16 model was already uncertain.

A note on the term high-entropy position: at a high-entropy position, the full-precision model spreads probability across many roughly comparable next-token candidates instead of concentrating it on one. In plain terms, the model has not made up its mind yet about what to say next. Low-entropy positions are the opposite: one token (a digit, an operator, the next step of an arithmetic chain) dominates and the rest of the distribution is essentially zero.

Two things happen at once at high-entropy positions. First, the logit gap between the top token and a hesitation token like “Wait” is small, so a modest perturbation to the logits can flip which token is sampled. Second, hesitation tokens themselves are 2 to 4× more likely to appear among the top-20 candidates at high-entropy positions than at low-entropy ones. Quantization noise has its largest effect exactly at these uncertain positions, so the quantized model is much more likely to sample a “Wait” or “But”, open a new reasoning branch, and overwrite the answer it had already found.

Confirming the diagnosis

If hesitation tokens at high-entropy positions really drive the failure, then suppressing them at decode time should shorten chains, shrink the overthinking error bucket specifically, and leave the other error categories alone. We curate a set $\mathcal S$ of 50 overthinking markers (“Wait”, “But”, “Alternatively”, “perhaps”, “maybe”, “however”, “reconsider”, “backtrack”, “wrong”, ...) and subtract a fixed penalty $\lambda > 0$ from their logits at every decoding step:

$$ z'_t(v) = z_t(v) - \lambda \;\;\text{if}\;\; v \in \mathcal S, \quad z'_t(v) = z_t(v) \;\;\text{otherwise.} $$

We use this penalty as a diagnostic, not as a proposed inference method. If our diagnosis is right, suppressing exactly these tokens should produce a specific, predictable change, and suppressing other token lists (random, lowest-KL math tokens) should produce nothing or the opposite. Both controls are run below.

We sweep $\lambda \in [0.5, 4.0]$ over four token lists of equal size (50 tokens each): our overthinking markers, the 50 highest-KL tokens, the 50 lowest-KL tokens, and 50 randomly selected tokens. Results are averaged over six quantization configurations and five benchmarks on Qwen-1.5B.

The overthinking markers list shortens CoT by 12 to 23% at every $\lambda$ we tried, while preserving or improving accuracy. It owns the upper-left of the Pareto frontier. Random tokens have negligible effect and stay near the no-penalty baseline. The lowest-KL math tokens are catastrophic: CoT grows by up to 41% and accuracy drops by up to 9.5%. Suppressing the tokens that compute the answer destroys reasoning, exactly as one would predict.

A symmetric sanity check: boosting the same overthinking markers (negative $\lambda$) inflates CoT by up to 445% and drops accuracy by up to 34%. Random and lowest-KL tokens stay flat. The lever is real, and it points in both directions.

The effect generalizes. Across all five models and all PTQ settings, the penalty reduces CoT length by 4.1 to 28.0% on average while preserving or improving accuracy. On MATH-500 under 3-bit AWQ, overthinking errors drop from 139 to 58 (a 58% reduction), without inflating the other error categories. Exactly what the diagnostic predicts.

Takeaways

  • A specific characterization of how PTQ degrades reasoning models. Not a generic capability hit. PTQ inflates overthinking errors specifically, up to 7.3× in absolute count on MATH-500 under 3-bit AWQ.
  • A token-level localization of the damage. KL divergence between BF16 and quantized output distributions concentrates on hesitation tokens at high-entropy positions (ρ = 0.92 between position-level KL and BF16 next-token entropy).
  • A diagnostic that confirms the story. A 50-token logit penalty cuts CoT length by 12 to 23% while preserving or improving accuracy. Random and lowest-KL controls do not.
  • A reusable analysis recipe. Token-level KL between a reference model and a compressed variant could serve as a general diagnostic for which aspects of generation are most vulnerable to compression, whether that compression is quantization, pruning, distillation, or low-rank approximation.

Caveats. The set $\mathcal S$ is curated for English reasoning models. The penalty $\lambda$ is fixed per decoding step rather than adapted to local entropy. Our evaluation is restricted to math, coding, and science benchmarks. Whether the same overthinking signature appears in agentic or open-domain reasoning is open.
